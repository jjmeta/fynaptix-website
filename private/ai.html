04 // BEAR CASE
The Bubble Debate
The revenue gap
The AI industry is burning an estimated $400B+ a year in infrastructure spend against $50–60B of direct AI revenue generated — a gap that keeps widening as hyperscaler capex guidance keeps rising, with the top-5 hyperscaler total now pegged at $775–800B for 2026. The BIS's June 2026 annual report names an AI-capex bust as one of its top three risks to global financial stability.
Rates are now the transmission mechanism
A late-July chip selloff has given way to a rate-driven one: the 30-year Treasury hit 5.32% on Aug 18, the semiconductor index (SOXX) fell nearly 5% in a single session, and CoreWeave — one of the most debt-heavy AI infrastructure names — dropped 12%. NVIDIA (-2.34%) and AMD (-4.27%) fell too despite no company-specific bad news, evidence a debt-funded buildout is now directly exposed to financing-cost risk, not just AI-demand risk.
Off-balance-sheet obligations turning concrete
Moody's flags ~$662B of signed-but-not-commenced data-center lease commitments sitting off hyperscaler balance sheets, and debt funding of the buildout has risen from 9% of capex in FY24 to 32% over the trailing twelve months (FactSet). NVIDIA's own Aug 10 response — $500B of financing MOUs with Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to move AI infrastructure onto institutional balance sheets — shows how much outside capital the next leg of the buildout now requires. Oracle remains the starkest case: ~$70B of FY2027 capex funded by a fresh $40B of debt and equity, a BBB- downgrade, and an FY26 cash outflow that already ran $23.7B ahead of what the company generated.
Circular financing
Interlocking vendor-equity stakes and take-or-pay compute deals among NVIDIA, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, Oracle and CoreWeave — echoing the Lucent/Nortel vendor-financing dynamics of 1999–2001 — make end-demand look larger and more independent than it is. Analysts peg the web of circular deals at over $800B; CNBC's Jim Cramer has repeatedly compared the pattern to the dot-com bubble, and the BIS flags a "circular financing collapse" as a top-three global financial-stability risk.
Depreciation clock
Hyperscalers depreciate AI hardware over 5–6 years, but bears argue the chips' real economic life is closer to 2–3 years — an estimated ~$176B understatement of depreciation across 2026–2028 that flatters reported earnings, with Oracle's earnings overstated by an estimated 26.9% and Meta's by 20.8% by 2028 on this analysis. Michael Burry has expanded his short book beyond NVIDIA and Palantir to include Nebius, Micron and the semiconductor ETF, calling current pricing "what the top of a boom looks like." Counterpoint: NVIDIA and CoreWeave point to secondary-market pricing and long-term contracts as evidence older silicon stays productive longer than the bear case assumes.
Layoffs while capex soars
Oracle cut ~21,000 jobs and is now guiding to ~$70B of FY2027 capex funded substantially by new debt, even as Microsoft cut another ~4,800 in July. Tech-sector layoffs are running at a 20-year high. The juxtaposition — record infrastructure spend, shrinking headcount, and companies like Meta disclosing their own AI systems breaching internal test environments — is fueling the argument that AI economics are being funded by cost-cutting elsewhere in the business, not by AI revenue itself.
Concentration everywhere
AI is simultaneously the market's largest index weight, VC's largest allocation (OpenAI and Anthropic alone took 43% of a record $510B global H1 2026 venture total), and this hub's connective thread — a drawdown would hit robotics, nuclear, storage, and quantum sentiment at once, as the Aug 18 rate-driven selloff already demonstrated across chip names. Counterpoint: infrastructure vendors keep posting record quarters (TSMC's 2nm demand already exceeding 3nm, Broadcom's reiterated $100B+ FY2027 AI semiconductor guide) — some strategists call the bear case premature given actual demand signals.
