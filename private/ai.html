04 // BEAR CASE
The Bubble Debate
The revenue gap
The industry has now committed an estimated $3–4T to AI infrastructure against an annualized return requirement of roughly $600–800B in new profit (up from Sequoia's original $200B framework in late 2023), while actual AI-attributable revenue runs an estimated $50–150B a year — a gap that keeps widening as the top-5 hyperscaler 2026/FY27 capex total sits at $825–855B. For context, cloud computing's capex-to-revenue ratio at the same point in its adoption curve (2011) was roughly 2.4:1, far below today's multiple. The BIS's June 2026 annual report still names an AI-capex bust as one of its top three risks to global financial stability.
AI's own architects sound the alarm
Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published "We Must Pace the Frontier" on Sept 12, arguing AI capability growth has accelerated "drastically faster" since summer 2026 — driven mainly by AI's growing ability to build the next generation of AI — and proposing independent safety evaluation, inter-lab coordination and international cooperation. OpenAI's Sam Altman and DeepMind's Demis Hassabis publicly backed the call; days later Palantir's Alex Karp went further, telling CNBC that frontier labs may need to be nationalized over "unlimited risk." The market reaction was real — chip names sold off while Meta, Alphabet and Microsoft rose — but skeptics note that a company mid-prep for a ~$2T IPO calling for the industry to slow down is also a moat-building and regulatory-capture argument, not purely an act of altruism.
Rates are the transmission mechanism — but strength can still win the argument
The 30-year Treasury eased slightly to ~5.36% on Sept 11 after touching its highest level since 2007 — still elevated as AI-linked debt issuance adds to bond-supply pressure. BofA estimates the surge in AI-related corporate debt sales has added roughly 0.3 percentage points to 10-year rates this year, and Nomura pegs hyperscaler borrowing alone at ~25% of net Treasury note/bond issuance to private investors. But Oracle's Sept 10 beat-and-raise, which sent the stock up ~15% on the week and has largely held since, kept the fundamental-demand side of the argument intact — a reminder the debate can swing hard in either direction week to week.
Off-balance-sheet obligations turning concrete
Moody's flags ~$662B of signed-but-not-commenced data-center lease commitments sitting off hyperscaler balance sheets, with debt funding of the buildout holding near 32% of capex over the trailing twelve months (FactSet). Oracle remains the starkest case on the balance sheet — still BBB- (one notch above junk) with FY26 free cash flow at -$23.7B and S&P still modeling a ~$42B FY27 deficit — even though Sept 10's Q1 FY2027 results (revenue +30%, Cloud Infrastructure +121%) were genuinely strong, and Larry Ellison canceling a planned $7.5B stock sale reads as a bullish insider signal. The credit risk and the operating momentum are both real; they haven't yet reconciled.
Circular financing
Nvidia is reportedly in talks to anchor-invest up to $10B in Anthropic's own IPO — a chipmaker taking a stake in the public offering of one of its largest customers — on top of the existing $105B OpenAI Ohio data-center financing guarantee. Michael Burry has called Nvidia's revenue "majority circularly financed" and built shorts on Oracle, Micron and Nebius plus March 2027 puts on the semiconductor ETF, though he said this month he "reduced every position" in September, trimming (not closing) that book — a partial de-risking worth noting rather than a reversal of conviction. One counterpoint: Broadcom's new 3.5-gigawatt Google TPU deal for Anthropic is real, non-Nvidia infrastructure being built — evidence some of this demand is diversifying rather than purely circulating.
Depreciation clock
Hyperscalers depreciate AI hardware over 5–6 years; bears argue the real economic life is closer to 2–3 years, understating depreciation by an estimated ~$176B across 2026–2028 and flattering reported earnings (Oracle by an estimated 26.9%, Meta by 20.8%, by 2028 on this analysis). Burry has called current pricing "what the top of a boom looks like," even as he trims position sizes generally. Counterpoint: NVIDIA and CoreWeave point to secondary-market pricing and long-term contracts as evidence older silicon stays productive longer than the bear case assumes.
Layoffs while capex soars
Layoffs.fyi's tracker showed 128,536 tech workers cut across 299 companies in 2026 through Sept 10 — already exceeding all of 2025 — while a broader tracker (SkillSyncer, different methodology) counts 210,741 workers across 383 layoff events through Sept 20. xAI itself cut roughly 500 data-annotation staff (a third of that team) on Sept 12 as part of a stated pivot to "specialist AI tutors." The juxtaposition — record infrastructure spend, record layoffs, and AI labs restructuring their own workforces — keeps fueling the argument that AI economics lean partly on cost-cutting elsewhere, not purely on AI revenue.
Concentration everywhere
AI is simultaneously the market's largest index weight, VC's largest allocation (OpenAI and Anthropic alone took over 40% of a record $510B global H1 2026 venture total, and AI took 86% of all US venture dollars in H1 per PitchBook), and this hub's connective thread — a drawdown would hit robotics, nuclear, storage and quantum sentiment at once. NVIDIA now sits at the center of deals spanning chips (TSMC, Broadcom), model hosting (Hugging Face), enterprise software (Palantir) and a potential anchor stake in Anthropic's IPO — a single company's balance sheet increasingly touches nearly every layer of this page, even as customers like Anthropic simultaneously build Google TPU alternatives. Counterpoint: infrastructure vendors keep posting record quarters and Oracle's Sept 10 beat is a live example — some strategists call the bear case premature given actual demand signals.
